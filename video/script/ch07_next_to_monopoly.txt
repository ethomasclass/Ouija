As for William Fuld, in 1927 he was up on the roof of his new factory, the one he said the board had told him to build, watching a flagpole go up. He fell, and he died. His family kept the company going for almost forty years.

Then, in 1966, they sold the Ouija board to Parker Brothers. The Monopoly company. Headquartered in Salem, Massachusetts. Yes, that Salem. The town of the 1692 witch trials. I could not make that up if I tried.

Now the Ouija board sits on toy-store shelves, right next to Monopoly and Clue. And in 1967, two million boards sold. More than Monopoly.

But look at who's playing now. Not courting couples. Not grieving mothers. Kids. Teenagers at sleepovers, lights off, flashlight on, whispering, "Is anybody there?"

And notice why they're playing. Kids aren't trying to reach Grandpa. They're trying to get scared. The spooky part isn't a side effect anymore. It's the whole point.

Parker Brothers said it was just a game. Which made it the perfect setup for what came next.
