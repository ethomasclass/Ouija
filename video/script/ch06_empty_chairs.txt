And then the world gave millions of people a reason to need that doorway.

In 1917, America enters World War One. More than a hundred and sixteen thousand Americans die. And in 1918, a flu pandemic sweeps the planet. It kills around six hundred seventy-five thousand people in the United States alone, and at least fifty million worldwide. Most of the victims of both are young. They leave behind parents. Wives. Sweethearts. Children.

So, just like after the Civil War, Spiritualism comes roaring back.

In Britain, a famous physicist, Sir Oliver Lodge, publishes a book of messages he believed came from his son Raymond, killed in the war. And Arthur Conan Doyle, the man who invented Sherlock Holmes, the most logical detective in fiction, loses his own son, Kingsley, in 1918. Doyle spends the rest of his life traveling the world, preaching that the dead are still with us.

And Ouija boards sell like never before. Mothers and wives buy them to reach sons and husbands who aren't coming home. William Fuld's "big business" arrives. It happens again in the next war. In 1944, a single New York department store sold fifty thousand boards in five months.

So picture one house around 1920. In the parlor, a teenager and her date, knees touching, giggling over the board. Upstairs, her mother, with the very same kind of board, trying to talk to the son she lost in France.

Same object. Two completely different stories.
