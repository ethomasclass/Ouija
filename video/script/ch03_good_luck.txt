1890. Baltimore. A businessman named Charles Kennard and a lawyer named Elijah Bond start the Kennard Novelty Company. Novelty. As in, a toy.

They need a name. The story goes that Bond's sister-in-law, Helen Peters, said to be a strong medium, asked the board itself. The pointer spelled O-U-I-J-A. And it said the word meant "good luck."

Helen, it turns out, was wearing a locket that night, with a picture of a woman and the name "Ouija" next to it. Probably the popular novelist Ouida. So the spirits might have just... read her necklace.

Then came the company's favorite legend. The patent office won't approve the board unless it works. If it can spell the examiner's name, which they don't know, they get their patent. The pointer spells his name. Patent granted, February 10th, 1891.

Did that really happen? We only have the inventors' word. But here's what we know for sure. The patent doesn't say one word about spirits. It's filed as a "Toy or Game."

And the first ads promise both. The Ouija answers questions "about the past, present and future with marvelous accuracy." It's also "a never-failing amusement." Price: a dollar fifty.

Hold that thought. Toy, or spirit? Even the people selling it wouldn't say.

Soon a young employee named William Fuld takes over and makes it his own. When a reporter asked if he believed in it, Fuld said: "I should say not. I'm no spiritualist. I'm a Presbyterian."
