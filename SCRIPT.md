# Good Luck: How the Ouija Board Went from Romantic to Scary

**15 Minute History** · narration script · 2,368 words · about 13:23

**Driving question (asked in the cold open, answered in the last chapter):** How did a game for sweethearts become the object people are afraid to touch?
**Answer:** the board never changed; the story around it did. Every generation put its biggest feeling into the pointer (love, then grief, then fun, then fear), and the ideomotor effect means the pointer goes where the players' own hands take it. *The Exorcist* didn't invent the fear, it gave everyone the same story at once, and the toy company eventually sold that story too.

## 0:00 | Two Pictures

May 1st, 1920. The cover of The Saturday Evening Post, one of the most-read magazines in America.

Norman Rockwell paints a young couple sitting face to face. Their knees are touching. A board rests across both their laps, and their fingertips share one little wooden pointer. It's sweet. It's a little flirty. Basically, it's a date.

Now jump ahead fifty-three years. December 1973. In movie theaters across America, a 12-year-old girl finds the same kind of board in her basement. She makes a new friend on it. She calls him Captain Howdy. And by the end of the movie, two priests are fighting for her soul.

Same board. Same alphabet. Same YES and NO in the corners. In 1920, it's what you bring on a date. Today, plenty of people won't even keep one in the house.

So here's the question I want to answer today: How did a game for sweethearts become the object people are afraid to touch?

One warning before we start. This wasn't a light switch. For most of its life, people used the Ouija board for love, for fun, for grief and for fear, all at the same time. But the story people told about it changed. And we're going to find out who changed it.

And it starts with some knocking.

## 1:20 | Knock Once for Yes

If you watched our Reform Era video, you've met the Fox sisters. 1848, upstate New York. Two girls say they hear knocking in their house, and the knocks answer questions. Once for yes. Silence for no.

That sparks Spiritualism, the belief that the living can talk to the dead. And after the Civil War kills hundreds of thousands of young men, millions of grieving families want very badly to believe it.

But there's a problem. Talking to spirits is slow. Someone calls out the alphabet, A, B, C, D, and waits for a knock on the right letter. Then starts over for the next one. Imagine texting, except you have to scroll through the whole alphabet for every single letter.

So people built shortcuts. Tables that tipped and tilted. A little heart-shaped wooden platform on wheels, with a pencil poking through it, called a planchette. Rest your fingers on it, and it writes.

Then, in 1886, the Associated Press sends a story to newspapers across the country. Spiritualists in Ohio have a new gadget: a "talking board." The alphabet is printed right on it. Numbers. "Yes" and "no." Nobody has to call out letters anymore. The spirits can just point.

And a few businessmen in Baltimore read about it and think: we could sell this.

## 2:30 | Good Luck

1890. Baltimore. A businessman named Charles Kennard gathers some investors, including a lawyer named Elijah Bond, and starts the Kennard Novelty Company. Novelty. As in, a toy.

They need a name. And the story goes that Bond's sister-in-law, Helen Peters, who was said to be a strong medium, asked the board itself. The pointer spelled out O-U-I-J-A. And when they asked what that meant, it answered: "Good luck."

You may have heard that "Ouija" is French "oui" plus German "ja." Yes, yes. That explanation came later. And Helen, it turns out, was wearing a locket that night, with a picture of a woman and the name "Ouija" next to it. It was probably Ouida, a popular novelist she admired. So the spirits might have just... read her necklace.

Next came the patent, and with it the company's favorite legend. The patent office won't approve the board unless they prove it works. So the chief examiner sets a test: if this thing can spell my name, which you don't know, you get your patent. They sit down. The pointer spells his name. And on February 10th, 1891, the patent is granted.

Did it really happen? We only have the inventors' side of the story. And Bond was a patent lawyer, so he might just have known the man's name. But here's what we know for sure. The patent itself doesn't say one word about spirits. It's filed as a "Toy or Game."

That same month, the first ads appear, from a toy shop in Pittsburgh. The Ouija answers questions "about the past, present and future with marvelous accuracy." It's "a never-failing amusement and recreation for all the classes." Price: a dollar fifty.

Hold that thought. From day one, the Ouija board is sold as two things at once: a mysterious link to the unknown, and a party game.

Soon a young employee named William Fuld takes over the business and makes it his own. He later said the board gave him advice: "Prepare for big business." And when a reporter asked if he actually believed in it, Fuld said: "I should say not. I'm no spiritualist. I'm a Presbyterian."

## 4:26 | Knees Touching

So who was buying it? A lot of young couples.

Think about dating around 1900. Rules everywhere. A young man and a young woman couldn't just be alone together. Parents watched. Neighbors talked.

Now look at how you play Ouija. It takes two people. You sit facing each other, with the board across both your laps. Knees touching. Lights low. Fingertips on the same little pointer, close enough to brush. Then you take turns asking questions. Does he like me? Will we get married? How many children will we have?

It's a parlor game with a built-in excuse to touch. And if the board says yes... well, who's going to argue with the spirits?

By 1920, America is in a full-blown Ouija craze. One newspaper reported that at the University of Michigan, the board had "succeeded the Bible and the prayer-book in fraternity houses."

And the summer before, Norman Rockwell went to a dance hall in Potsdam, New York. He noticed couples sitting knee to knee, moving little heart-shaped pointers around Ouija boards. That's where the magazine cover came from. A couple, a board, and a question that probably isn't about ghosts at all.

## 5:29 | Patience Worth

But at the very same time, other people were taking the board dead seriously.

July 8th, 1913. St. Louis, on a hot summer night. A housewife named Pearl Curran and her friend Emily Hutchings sit down at a Ouija board. Pearl's mother sits beside them, writing down each letter. And the pointer spells out:

"Many moons ago I lived. Again I come. Patience Worth my name."

Patience Worth said she was an Englishwoman from the 1600s. And she had a lot to say. Over the next twenty-four years, Pearl produced around four million words in Patience's name. Novels, plays, thousands of poems. A review in The New York Times found "flashes of genius" in her work. After a while, Pearl didn't even need the board anymore. She just spoke the words out loud as they came to her.

So was it really a ghost? Most researchers think Patience Worth came out of Pearl's own mind. Which, honestly, is impressive enough. But to her readers, it looked like proof.

Notice what's happening. The same board that's a flirting game in somebody's parlor is, in St. Louis, a doorway to a voice from three hundred years ago.

## 6:32 | Empty Chairs

And then the world gave millions of people a reason to need that doorway.

In 1917, America enters World War One. More than a hundred and sixteen thousand Americans die. And in 1918, a flu pandemic sweeps the planet. It kills around six hundred seventy-five thousand people in the United States alone, and at least fifty million worldwide. Most of the victims of both are young. They leave behind parents. Wives. Sweethearts. Children.

So, just like after the Civil War, Spiritualism comes roaring back.

In Britain, a famous physicist, Sir Oliver Lodge, publishes a book of messages he believed came from his son Raymond, killed in the war. And Arthur Conan Doyle, the man who invented Sherlock Holmes, the most logical detective in fiction, loses his own son, Kingsley, in 1918. Doyle spends the rest of his life traveling the world, preaching that the dead are still with us.

And Ouija boards sell like never before. Mothers and wives buy them to reach sons and husbands who aren't coming home. William Fuld's "big business" arrives. It happens again in the next war. In 1944, a single New York department store sold fifty thousand boards in five months.

So picture one house around 1920. In the parlor, a teenager and her date, knees touching, giggling over the board. Upstairs, her mother, with the very same kind of board, trying to talk to the son she lost in France.

Same object. Two completely different stories.

## 8:00 | Next to Monopoly

As for William Fuld, in 1927 he was up on the roof of his new factory, the one he said the board had told him to build, watching a flagpole go up. He fell, and he died. His family kept the company going for almost forty years.

Then, in 1966, they sold the Ouija board to Parker Brothers. The Monopoly company. Headquartered in Salem, Massachusetts. Yes, that Salem. The town of the 1692 witch trials. I could not make that up if I tried.

Now the Ouija board sits on toy-store shelves, right next to Monopoly and Clue. And in 1967, two million boards sold. More than Monopoly.

But look at who's playing now. Not courting couples. Not grieving mothers. Kids. Teenagers at sleepovers, lights off, flashlight on, whispering, "Is anybody there?"

And notice why they're playing. Kids aren't trying to reach Grandpa. They're trying to get scared. The spooky part isn't a side effect anymore. It's the whole point.

Parker Brothers said it was just a game. Which made it the perfect setup for what came next.

## 8:58 | Captain Howdy

Rewind to 1949. In Maryland, just outside Washington, D.C., a family says something is wrong with their teenage son. Strange noises. Furniture moving on its own. According to some accounts, it all started after his aunt, a Spiritualist, taught him to use a Ouija board.

The family turns to the Catholic Church, and priests perform an exorcism, much of it in St. Louis. Yes, St. Louis again. A college student at Georgetown named William Peter Blatty reads about it in The Washington Post. He never forgets it.

Twenty-two years later, he turns it into a novel. And in December 1973, it becomes a movie: The Exorcist.

In the movie, 12-year-old Regan finds a Ouija board in the basement and plays with it alone. She tells her mom about her new friend. "I make the questions," she says, "and he does the answers."

People lined up around the block to see it. Some fainted in the theaters. It became one of the biggest movies ever made.

The Ouija historian Robert Murch compares it to the movie Psycho: "No one was afraid of showers until that scene."

Now, The Exorcist didn't invent the fear. Some religious leaders had warned people away from Spiritualism and talking boards for decades. What the movie did was hand that fear to everyone at once. Millions of people. One story. And now, the board had a villain.

After that, Ouija boards turn up in horror movie after horror movie. And in 2001, in Alamogordo, New Mexico, a church held a bonfire and burned Ouija boards, along with Harry Potter books and Disney's Snow White.

But here's the twist. Who was selling the fear? In 2014, a horror movie called Ouija hit theaters. One of the companies that made it was Hasbro, which owns Parker Brothers. It cost somewhere between five and eight million dollars, and it made over a hundred million. The toy company made a horror movie about its own toy.

And just six years earlier, Hasbro had put out a pink Ouija board, aimed at girls ages eight and up. Parents were furious. Same company, same years: the Ouija board as a slumber-party toy, and the Ouija board as a doorway for demons.

## 10:57 | Who Moves It?

So, back to our question. How did a game for sweethearts become the object people are afraid to touch?

Look at the board. Put one from 1891 next to one you could buy today. The alphabet in two curved rows. The numbers underneath. YES and NO in the corners. GOOD BYE at the bottom. The board barely changed.

The story around it did. And the story depended on whose hands were on the pointer.

Couples asked about love, and the board talked about love. Grieving mothers asked about their sons, and it talked about their sons. Kids at sleepovers wanted to be scared, and after 1973, everybody knew exactly what a scary Ouija board sounds like.

Remember when I asked you to hold that thought? Toy or spirit? Who's actually moving the pointer?

Scientists had an answer before the Ouija board was even invented. In 1852, a British doctor named William Carpenter described what he called ideomotor action: your muscles can move because of an idea, without you deciding to move them. A year later, the scientist Michael Faraday tested Spiritualists' tipping tables, and showed that the people around the table were pushing it themselves, without realizing it.

And in 2012, researchers at the University of British Columbia blindfolded people at a Ouija board. Their partner quietly let go of the pointer, so each player was really alone. When players thought they were just guessing at a yes-or-no question, their spoken answers were right about half the time. Pure chance. But through the board, they were right about sixty-five percent of the time. The board knew a little more than they thought they did. Because the board was them.

So who turned the Ouija board from romantic to scary? Not one person. Not even one movie. We did. Every generation put its biggest feelings into that little pointer. Love. Grief. Fun. Fear. And the pointer went right where our hands took it.

That's what people do when something seems to move on its own. We reach for whatever explanation our own time hands us. For a couple in 1920, it was romance. For a mother who lost her son in 1918, it was her boy, still out there somewhere. After 1973, it was a demon.

When Helen Peters asked the board what its name meant, it told her: "Good luck."

Turns out, that was up to whoever was holding it.

## Fact-check flags

- **Patent-office demonstration:** a Kennard/Bond family story, passed down through Ouija historian Robert Murch. No independent record. The narration hedges it ("the story goes," "we only have the inventors' side").
- **Helen Peters and the locket:** also from the family account via Murch (Smithsonian). Ouida as the source of the name is a likely explanation, not proven.
- **Fuld's "Prepare for big business":** Fuld's own claim (American Heritage); the narration says "he later said."
- **Fuld quote:** "I should say not. I'm no spiritualist. I'm a Presbyterian." Wording is consistent across sources; the original interview date wasn't confirmed.
- **"Succeeded the Bible and the prayer-book in fraternity houses":** reported by the *New York Tribune* about the University of Michigan (American Heritage). Narration says "one newspaper reported."
- **Patience Worth's output:** "around four million words" follows American Heritage; other sources describe seven novels plus plays and thousands of poems. The NYT "flashes of genius" quote is from a review cited by American Heritage.
- **1949 case:** location is given variously as Cottage City or Mount Rainier, Md., and the boy's age as 13 or 14, so the narration says "just outside Washington" and "teenage son." The aunt-and-Ouija origin is from accounts of the case, hedged with "according to some accounts."
- **Rockwell cover:** the narration only describes what's certain (knees touching, board on both laps, shared pointer). Check the painting before adding anything about who's looking where.
- **"Two priests":** the film's exorcism is performed by Fathers Merrin and Karras.
- **1918 flu:** ~675,000 U.S. deaths is the CDC figure; "at least 50 million worldwide" is the common estimate (range 17–100 million). WWI: ~116,000 U.S. military deaths.
- **Kingsley Doyle** died in October 1918 of pneumonia following his war wounds. Doyle had publicly embraced Spiritualism in 1916, before Kingsley's death, so the narration doesn't say the loss *caused* his belief.
- **Ouija (2014):** budget reported as $5–8 million; box office ~$103.6 million.
- **Pink Ouija:** released by Hasbro in 2008 for ages 8+; boycott and Toys "R" Us pull-back followed.
